# Cold Glitter Creek

This repository holds the game in two versions: the original three.js web version (`index.html`, `src/`, the
"Paydirt Hollow" described below) and the Godot 4.7.2 port, **Cold Glitter Creek** (`godot/`), which is where work
happens now. `godot/PORT.md` is the port's plan and progress log.

## Working on it as a team

1. Install [Git LFS](https://git-lfs.com) once, then clone: `git lfs install` and `git clone https://github.com/JJLSU/CGC`.
   Models (`.glb`), images and sounds come down through Git LFS.
2. Link Godot's models folder to the shared one (Windows, from the repo folder, in Command Prompt):
   `mklink /J godot\assets\models assets\models`
   (macOS/Linux: `ln -s ../../assets/models godot/assets/models`)
3. Open `godot/project.godot` in Godot 4.7.2. The first open re-imports every asset (a few minutes); Godot keeps that
   cache in `godot/.godot/`, which isn't in the repository.
4. Not in the repository, kept in the team's shared drive: the raw Meshy downloads (`assets/models/meshy/`) and the
   reference images (`assets/refs/`). The game doesn't need either.
5. Never commit API keys: the Meshy scripts in `tools/` read `MESHY_API_KEY` from your environment.

# Paydirt Hollow

A hard-scrabble gold-rush tale in paper. You're a down-on-your-luck prospector with a claim, a gold pan and not much else, on a homestead down the lane from **Paydirt Hollow**, a mining town gone to seed since the first strike played out. Pan the creeks for colour, sell it at the assay office, hire the town's folk to run your works, and strike it rich. (The game was called Paperfjord; saves carry over.)

## Paydirt Hollow (the gold rush)

- **NEXT:** a bold one-line card at the top left always says the most useful thing to do now, in a few words. Hunger comes first ("Hungry! Press F to eat", a red nudging card). After that it walks the gold road: pick up the opening glint, sell gold at the assay office, buy the root cellar and then the bunkhouse when you can afford them, mend the cheapest run-down building in town whenever your poke covers it ("Mend the barber shop in town ($15)", on Main Street or any street the town has opened), build a workbench, make a pickaxe, find iron ore (rusty grey rocks), build a forge, smelt iron, make an iron pickaxe, then mine gold quartz. Once you have the iron pick, it points you at the day's doings when there are any: the Sunday meeting that morning, the County Fair, a barn dance tonight, or the fireworks over a finished town. The hunger notes are short now too ("Hungry! Eat something (F).", "STARVING! Eat now (F).").
- **Gold quartz needs an iron pickaxe** (like silver): a plain pick or an axe just bounces off ("Too hard! Needs an IRON pickaxe"). Panning gets you started; the iron pick is what gets you rich.
- **Mended buildings come back to life:** each building you mend gets its own person out on the boardwalk by the door, and a barrel of red and yellow flowers on the other side:
  - **the general store:** the storekeeper in a long white apron;
  - **the boarding house:** the landlady;
  - **the Golden Garter:** Miss Dolly Valentine, the hostess, in a short ruffled red dress over black stockings with red garters, a laced black corset and a lace neckline. She has long blonde curls with a red flower, painted lips and a beauty mark. She stands with a hand on her cocked hip, crooking a finger at passers-by, and has a word for you once a day ("Buy a girl a drink, sugar?");
  - **the livery:** a big liveryman with his sleeves rolled up;
  - **the blacksmith's:** Big Amos, broad as a door, hammering away;
  - **the church:** the parson in black with his book.
- **The folk of the county got a makeover:**
  - **Paper dolls can be curvy** (a nipped waist and a bodice) **or burly** (shoulders wider than the waist, a deep chest, thick arms). Faces can be glamorous: coloured lids, long flicked lashes, painted cupid's-bow lips and a beauty mark. Hair can be long and curled (one thin fall down the back, flush with the head), with optional rolled sleeves, stockings, a short skirt and a flower in the hair.
  - **The younger women each have their own look:** Hattie the quarrywoman is a strapping redhead with her sleeves rolled. Clementine is the sweetheart with honey curls, pink lips and a lace neckline. Nell is a sultry brunette with a beauty mark and an oxblood corset. Patience the collier is dark and dangerous in black, with wine-red lips.
  - **The can-can dancers** now wear short ruffled skirts, stockings and corsets.
  - **Silas, Big Jim, Otis and Luther are big, strong men**, and Silas, Jim and Luther have their sleeves rolled up over brawny forearms.
- **The first gold:** a new game opens on the rumour that brought you west ("with a pan, an old hatchet and forty cents"). A few steps from the cabin door, in the direction of town, a little heap of gold lies twinkling in the gravel. Press E ("[E] Pick up the glint in the gravel") for 2 pinches: a nugget the size of a pea and two fat flakes, and a note that tells you how the game goes: pan the creeks, break gold quartz, sell at the assay office in town. If you build something on top of it before you pick it up, it hops to clear ground nearby. Older saves skip it.
- **Two ways to get gold:** pan the shallows (below), or mine **gold quartz**. Gold quartz is a grey-white rock ledge shot through with rusty seams, gold wire and fat gold flakes, found here and there in the Redrock Breaks and the Bluepine Highlands. Break it with a pickaxe (8 strokes) for 1–2 pinches of gold. It's rare, unguarded, and grows back slowly.
- **Main Street comes alive:** the tinker's red wagon draws up at the head of Main Street, by the general store, on his visiting days (not at the end of your farm lane any more). The County Fair's judging table stands up the street in front of the church, facing back down it. On dance nights the barn dance (lanterns, fiddler and couples) is in the street before the livery barn.
- **The town** stands about 85 m from the homestead, on its own level ground in the home county, with its main street running straight out from your farm, so from home you look right down it to the church (src/town.js). It's a crooked main street of false-front buildings: the general store (its sign worn to "GEN RAL MDSE"), the assay office, a two-storey boarding house, the Golden Garter saloon and dance hall with its batwings nailed shut (its sign worn to "GOLD N GART R"), a livery barn and a blacksmith's. A leaning white church with its steeple askew closes the far end of the street, and canvas tents are pitched behind (folk stand at their doorways, outside the canvas). Each building is a printed false front with a stepped parapet and sign board, standing proud of a plain box body under a sway-backed shake roof, with a plank boardwalk at its feet. Paint peels in pale patches and the windows are boarded with crossed planks. The street is a long torn sheet of ochre dirt with wheel ruts, and a sign by the lane reads "PAYDIRT HOLLOW · Pop. 11 (and falling)". The cards have plain weathered backs, so no sign ever shows through mirrored from behind. The facades are painted per tier, so the town can be mended in the next phase.
- **The fingerpost:** at the farm end of the lane to town stands a weathered post stacked with hand-lettered arrow boards, one pointing the true way to Paydirt Hollow and one to the nearest stretch of each of the five wild lands, each with its distance in paces. The boards are lettered on both faces, so they read from either side.
- **Squatters' Row:** just outside town on the farm side, a ragged lane of scrap-board shanties under rusted tin roofs sits off a hand-painted sign. The shanties have stovepipes, a sack hung across the door and a red rag for a curtain, and patched canvas tents stand among them. The tents are folded paper A-frames: two printed canvas sheets leaning together on a ridge pole, with sewn seams, stitched-on patches and mud splashed up the hem. Each has triangular end walls (a dark doorway at the front with the flap tied back) and guy-ropes. The same tents stand behind Main Street. The down-and-out live here: all the folk who'll work for you, from the first day, with no mending or storehouse needed. Hire them on the Row. (Newcomers who come for the boarding house's rooms live in town instead.) You recruit them in town on the same terms as before. Once hired they walk out to your works by day and home to town at night. The settlers card says "lives in Paydirt Hollow". Every squatter now lives in a patched canvas wall tent (the scrap-board shanties are gone). **Folk turn in at night:** from late evening till before first light, the squatters go into their tents and your hired hands into the bunkhouse once they're home, and they step back out in the morning. In town too: late at night (from about half past nine till first light) the shopkeepers and the assayer shut up shop and go indoors, and the families on Squatters' Row turn in to their tents (their wants keep till morning). After dark every tent glows warm from within, a lantern lit inside, its patches and seams showing through the canvas with a faint flicker (one shared shader uniform, no extra lights). Only the night trades keep their doors: Miss Dolly and the gambler at the Golden Garter, and the Grand Hotel's clerk. Folk never stand inside one another: anyone closer than about an arm's length eases apart, and the bunkhouse spots are spread wider.
- **The town grows:** once every building on Main Street (and the church) is mended, folk put up **Second Street** alongside it: its own rutted dirt street and street-name post, and six tumbledown buildings to mend in turn (the miners' bank, barber shop, bakery, sheriff's jail, feed & grain and opera house). Mend all of those and **Third Street** goes up on the other side (telegraph office, Grand Hotel, dry goods, cooperage, stage stables and dance hall). All twelve buildings on Second and Third Street now wear their own models from the reference art, run-down and restored. Each one, once mended, gets a tin lantern on a post by its door; after dark they all glow warm down the street (one shared material, no extra lights). Once mended, the feed barn and the stage stables each get a hitching rail out front with a horse tied at it, nose to the rail, swishing its tail. A freight wagon is drawn up before the mended feed & grain, and the stage line's coach waits in the street before its stables (both solid: walk round them). **The bakery** on Second Street opens once it is mended: the baker stands out front in her white apron, and E at her door opens her counter. She sells pumpkin pie ($6), apple pie ($7), honey cake ($5) and a pan of biscuits ($3), and buys 2 sacks of flour for $5, 6 eggs for $4 and a pumpkin for $3. **The Miners' bank** opens once it is mended: Mrs. Vance, who keeps its ledger, stands by the door in her green walking dress, and E at the teller's window lets you deposit or withdraw $10 or $50. Every morning the bank adds 3% interest (at least $1 on $10 or more), with a note saying so. Your savings show in the ledger as a little iron safe, and they're safe from anything that happens to you out in the wilds. **The sheriff's jail** posts bounties once it is mended: press E at its door and the sheriff picks the nearest guarded den 60 to 320 m from town that still has its guardians. He tacks a WANTED poster to a post by the jail door (yellowed paper: the guardian's name, DEAD OR DRIVEN OFF, the reward), pencils the den onto your map and tells you how far and which way. The reward is about 1.4 times the guardians' total strength, at least $12. Beat every guardian there (blows, arrows or bullets), then come back to his door for the money. One bounty at a time; it's saved with the game. **The barber shop** opens once it is mended: the barber waits by the door in shirtsleeves and a red waistcoat. Press E for a shave and a haircut ($2, once a day). You stay spruced up till the next morning: the assayer pays a tenth more for your gold and silver, and the folk on Squatters' Row pay $1 more for what they want. **The feed & grain** opens once it is mended: the feed man stands by the big barn doors in his straw hat, and E opens his counter. A sack of laying mash costs $3: five mornings' worth, a scoop each morning (automatically, while you have some), and the hens lay two extra eggs tucked in the straw, collected with the rest. He also sells a sack of oats ($2, three feeds): the first time you ride each day, your horse eats a feed and runs a quarter faster all day (oats show in the ledger as a little sack). He also buys 4 corn for $3 and 3 wool for $5. Mash shows in the ledger as a little grain sack. **The Grand Hotel** on Third Street rents rooms once it is mended: its clerk in a plum frock coat waits by the door. From sundown, E takes a room for $4: you sleep through to morning as in your own bed, and wake fed (breakfast of ham and eggs), rested and with a spring in your step. **The telegraph office** on Third Street takes orders by wire once it is mended: its telegrapher in a green eyeshade stands by the door. Each morning's wire names a buyer back East and the one thing they want today, at a good price: pelts ($5 each, a St. Louis furrier), wool ($3), honey ($5), venison ($3), leather ($7), maple syrup ($6), charcoal ($2) or eggs ($1). Press E to sell every one you carry, once a day; the E prompt shows today's order and how many you have. **The dry goods** on Third Street opens once it is mended: its shopkeeper in a slate-blue bustle dress stands by the door, and E opens her counter. She sells ready-made what you'd otherwise make from hides and hours: a sheepskin coat ($18), leather boots ($14), a berry basket ($3), a lightning-bug jar ($2), and calico by the bolt ($2 for 4 fibre). She buys hides at $3. **The cooperage** on Third Street opens once it is mended: the cooper works at his bench by the door in a leather apron, and E opens his counter. Storehouse casks ($20, once) let your storehouse hold 45 of each thing from the works instead of 30. A rain barrel and pail ($6, once) bring your crops, apples and berries back a quarter sooner. He buys 6 wood for $3. **The stage stables** on Third Street run a coach once they are mended: the driver waits by the big doors. The stage goes to any landmark you've found out in the county ($3; E boards, Q picks the next stop, nearest first) and sets you down a short walk from it. Come back to that spot and press E to wave down the coach home to the stables ($3). Each ride takes about three quarters of an hour of the day. Not on horseback: stable your horse first. **The dance hall** on Third Street comes alive once it is mended: from dusk till past midnight, two couples (civilian models in re-dyed Sunday best) swing round in the street before its doors. A fiddler plays on its boardwalk, and within about 30 m you hear the reel (a fourth above the barn dance's tune, and a shade quicker). Press E at the door of an evening to dance a reel, once a night: you come out with a long spring in your step (sprinting barely tires you for a good while) and a little hungrier. About half an hour passes. **The opera house** opens once it is mended. A playbill on a post by its door names tonight's tall tale, a different one each night: Paul Bunyan and Babe the Blue Ox, Pecos Bill riding the cyclone, John Henry the steel-driving man, Davy Crockett and the frozen dawn, Mike Fink, Febold Feboldson and Sally Ann Thunder Whirlwind Crockett. From sundown to near midnight, E at the door buys a seat for $3, once a night. About an hour passes in the dark, and you come out at full health and stamina, not hungry, with the tale told in a note. At curtain time (sundown to about nine) a few theatre-goers in their best wait in a line along the boardwalk by its doors. Their lots are levelled and kept clear from the start, so the streets go up on open ground. When a new street goes up, word spreads in a note: folk are putting up Second (or Third) Street beside the old one. Which streets are open is saved.
- **Bunting over a mended street:** once every building on a street is mended (Main Street with its church, then Second and Third), three drooping cords of red, cream and blue paper pennants are strung across it from eave to eave. If raiders wreck a building there, the bunting comes down until it is mended again.
- **Shopkeepers look up as you pass:** come within a few paces of a mended building's keeper and they turn to watch you go by (never more than a quarter turn from the street), then ease back once you have gone on. The first time you pass each keeper in a day they call out a word of greeting ("Mornin'!", "Howdy!", "Fine day!", "Evenin'!" after supper), a little hand-lettered paper card that springs up over their head on its hinge and folds away.
- **Saturday-night fireworks:** once all three streets are mended, the town celebrates every Saturday evening. From the field behind the church, rockets go up one after another trailing sparks, and burst into rounds of little cut-paper stars (red, cream, gold, blue or pink) that open out, hang a moment, then drift down shrinking away, each with a soft far-off pop. It is one instanced mesh, run only while the show is on. After supper on those Saturdays the families of Squatters' Row walk over and stand about Main Street in a loose crowd, all looking up the street to the sky over the church, and go home again when the show is done.
- **The railroad (stage 1: the line):** the day all three of Paydirt Hollow's streets stand mended, the Overland & Western lays a rail line. A depot goes up past the church (a printed clapboard station house with its telegraph pole, a long plank platform and a PAYDIRT HOLLOW name board), and the track runs out from it across the county, through a whistle-stop in each of the five wild lands. Each stop has a plank platform beside the line on the outside of the bend and a name board you read from the track. The track is ties and iron rails laid on the ground, and runs on a timber trestle over water; it swings wide round the town and the homestead. Its right-of-way is surveyed from the start, so no tree or rock grows on it. 
- **The railroad (stage 2: the train):** the Overland & Western's little paper train runs the line on a timetable. A black 4-4-0 locomotive banded in brass, with a flared red stack, a cowcatcher, a lamp and big red drivers, pulls her tender, a rust-red boxcar and, at the back, a green passenger coach lettered OVERLAND & WESTERN with an open railed platform. Each car's flanks are printed card: the cab has an arched window and No. 7 in gold, the tender a brass band, rivets and O. & W. on a red panel, the boxcar upright boards, diagonal braces, a Z-braced sliding door and stencilled O. & W. No. 1871, and the coach a beadboard wainscot, gold pinstripes, a letterboard and a row of lamp-lit arched windows. She leaves the depot with a whistle mid-morning and again mid-afternoon, draws up at each whistle-stop for a while, waits at the far end of the line and runs home again. Smoke puffs from her stack while she runs, she chuffs as she goes (heard close by) and whistles leaving each station; anyone standing on the track is nudged off it. Out of sight she isn't drawn.
- **The railroad (stage 3: riding):** walk up to the coach wherever she's standing, at the depot or a whistle-stop, and press E to board for a dollar's fare. From the depot she leaves at once; you ride standing on the coach's back platform by the railing while the county rolls past, and the prompt tells you the next stop. Press E at any stop she draws up at to step down onto its platform: the quick way out to the Redrock Breaks, the Golden Prairie, the Bluepine Highlands, the Misty Fen or the Silver Birch Hollows, and home again.
- **The railroad (stage 4: freight):** the depot has a freight office at the end of its platform: press E there to ship your goods East at a steady price. Ten wood makes $6 of lumber for the Denver yards, ten stone $4, six bricks $7, six corn $5, three pumpkins $5, eight apples $4, three fleeces $6, two hides $7, two jars of honey $7, three sacks of flour $6, two jugs of syrup $8 and two hides of leather $9. You're paid on the bill of lading, and each shipment sets a crate out on the platform. The crates stack up there until the next train calls, when the freight hands swing them into the boxcar and she pulls out for the East (the crates waiting are saved with the game).
- **The railroad (stage 5: whistle-stop trading posts):** behind each whistle-stop's platform stands a little clapboard trading post with a hand-lettered sign, a barrel and crates by the door, a plank walk down to the platform and its keeper out front by day (indoors after dark). Ride out and press E at the door to trade. Each land sells what it has plenty of and pays well for what it's short of. Redrock Breaks: ore and drill steel, and $6 for a pie. Golden Prairie: wheat and buffalo leather, and $6 for a bar of iron. Bluepine Highlands: winter pelts and charcoal, and $7 for two sacks of flour. Misty Fen: fish and medicine root, and $5 for three coils of rope. Silver Birch Hollows: maple syrup and cords of birch, and $7 for two loaves.
- **The railroad (stage 6: the telegraph):** telegraph poles march beside the line, each a little off true, with a crossarm, two green glass insulators and two wires sagging between them. Each morning there's no order standing, the depot's telegraph clatters out a rush order from one of the whistle-stops: three pans of cornbread for the night shift at the Redrock diggings, eggs and milk for a prairie wedding, pies for the Bluepine logging camp, honey for the fever in the Misty Fen, iron for the Silver Birch sugar kettles and more. Each one is due within three days and pays $12 to $16, well over the usual price. Deliver it at that stop's trading post (it shows there as a telegram in the trades). The order stands in the Almanac (H) until it's filled. A lapsed order is lost, and a new one comes the next morning. The standing order is saved with the game.
- **Your own gold claim:** the keeper of the Redrock Breaks trading post is the district recorder too. For $25 ("File a mining claim") you can file on the ground a short walk back along the line from that whistle-stop. It's staked out at once: four corner stakes with red rag flags, a claim notice on a post (THE HOMESTEAD LODE), an old adit timbered into a heap of red rock, a windlass, an ore cart on a few yards of plank track and a rack of tools. Once a day, press E at the adit with a pickaxe to work the vein. It gives 2 pinches of gold, plus 1 more with an iron pick and another with a steel one, sometimes 1 extra, and about one day in three a nugget of silver. Sell them at the assay office. Ride the train out to it, or walk.
- **A hired hand on your claim:** once you've filed, the Redrock Breaks post can find you a hand for $40, a steady old Cornish miner out of work since the Comstock. He pitches his tent on the claim and works the vein every day, swinging his pick at the adit mouth by day and sleeping in his tent after dark. Each day he digs 2 or 3 pinches of gold, and now and then some silver. His takings wait in the ore cart, up to about five days' worth, so ride out now and then. Press E at the adit to have him tip the cart out for you. You can still work the vein yourself once a day as well. His takings are saved with the game.
- **A stamp mill on your claim:** with a hand taken on, the Redrock Breaks post will freight up a little five-stamp mill for $80. It goes up beside the adit, its water wheel and stamps thumping away by day (a dull thud you hear as you come near, the frame giving a little shudder with each one). It crushes the quartz fine, so your hand brings in two more pinches of gold a day, and the cart holds more before he stops, and you get one more pinch when you work the vein yourself.
- **Smoke over the town:** the mended smithy, bakery and cooperage keep a fire all day, and the boarding house and the Grand Hotel light their cookstoves at breakfast and supper. Paper smoke puffs rise from the building's own chimney when its model has one (found once by dropping rays over the roof), or else from a black tin stovepipe set on the back of the roof. They share the campfire smoke's three nearest slots, so they cost nothing extra. Every smoke puff (campfires, forge, camps and town alike) turns its full face to the lens and glows faintly cream of its own by day, so a puff seen against the sun or from high above stays cream, never grey.
- **Back lots:** behind each mended building stands the clutter of a working yard, by trade: a woodpile and a rain barrel behind the smithy, barrels behind the cooperage, hay behind the livery, the stage stables and the feed & grain, crates and barrels behind the saloon, the hotel and the stores. Each yard is seeded by building, so its things stay where they were, and they are solid to walk into.
- **Working beasts in town:** a patient ox stands in the traces of each freight wagon (before the mended general store and the feed & grain), and a donkey is tethered by the mended livery barn. They idle, ears and tails twitching, and are solid to walk into. At bedtime they are put up in the stables for the night, and so are the horses at the hitching rails of the feed & grain and the stage stables; all are back by morning. The mended Golden Garter has a hitching rail too, at the far end of its front: a late customer's horse stands there day and night, the one horse in town still out after bedtime.
- **Sunday meeting:** every seventh morning, once the church is mended, the dawn bell is followed by meeting. The families of Squatters' Row leave their tents (and their errands) and walk briskly up Main Street to the churchyard, where they stand in loose rows before the doors, facing in, until mid-morning, then wander home again. A toast tells you when it begins if you are near town. Once they are all in place, a slow plain hymn in three (written for the game) drifts out of the churchyard on a wheezy pump organ under a soft hum of voices, loudest at the church door and gone by about 40 m away.
- **Tumbleweeds:** on dry days in town and out in the Redrock Breaks, now and then a ball of dry brush (five ragged discs of pale straw card slotted together) comes bowling down the west wind, hopping and spinning. It slows and catches the wind again, fetches up against walls, posts and tents and is shoved round them, and rolls on past you and away. There are never more than two, and none in rain, at night or in green country. Old Blue can't abide them: when one bowls by within a dozen paces he barks and lopes off after it, snapping at its twigs for a few bounds, then gives it up and trots back to you (now and then, not for every one).
- **The lamplighter:** Main Street has six iron street lamps on tall posts. At dusk old Amos Teague comes out at the head of the street and walks down it lamp by lamp, stopping at each a moment to light it, then goes in at the church door for the night. The lamps are put out at sunup. Arrive after his round, or be far off when he makes it, and you find them all already lit. Each lit lamp casts a soft warm round of amber light with a ragged rim on the dirt beneath it. (The six posts, crossbars and caps are one merged mesh; only each lamp's glass and pool of light are its own, so they can be lit one by one.)
- **Squatters' Row grows with the town:** each new street raises four more tents in a second rank behind the first, opening onto the field, and a family stands outside each one. Over their heads a cream paper note with a red "!" turns slowly while they want something: apples, fish, a loaf, firewood, venison, honey, a hide, eggs, rope or berries. Press E beside them: if you have it, they pay $3 to $7. When you're already carrying what someone wants, their note turns into a pale sage card with a green tick, so you can see from the street who to call on. Both notes are printed on both faces, so they read as they turn. The next morning they want something else. By day they don't just stand about: now and then one strolls over to a neighbour's tent on their own side of the Row, stands a while for a word, and wanders home again (their note goes with them, and E works wherever they are). They never walk into one another.
- **Panning for gold:** wade into the pale shallows of any creek or pond and press E to pan for colour. The farmer swirls a tin pan in front of him for about 2½ seconds while the gravel washes out over the lip. Sometimes there's gold at the bottom: a pinch (now and then two) of bright flakes, with a gold sparkle. Usually it's "nothing but black sand". The home county's creeks are poor (about one pan in four or five). The Redrock Breaks and the Bluepine Highlands are much richer, and the Hollows a little.
- **The root cellar:** the first thing gold buys for your homestead. A surveyor's stake with a paper tag (ROOT CELLAR · dig here · $20) stands east of the farmhouse; press E there with $20. A grassy earth mound rises, with a fieldstone header, two slanted plank doors on iron strap hinges and a stovepipe vent. Every morning there's a basket of apples out on the doors: press E for 2 apples and a loaf of bread, once a day. The NEXT line suggests it when you have $20, and points you to it when you're hungry with nothing to eat.
- **The bunkhouse:** the homestead's second purchase. A stake tagged BUNKHOUSE · build here · $40 stands in the old camp ground west of the yard; press E there with $40. A long, low log bunkhouse goes up, with chinked log walls, a shake roof, a stovepipe, a plank door, two windows that glow after dark and a bench by the door. Your hired hands bed down there instead of trudging back to Squatters' Row, so they're at work sooner and longer: every work turns out a quarter more. Out front of the bunkhouse is a ring of stones with four split logs round it: at dusk the supper fire is lit and your hired hands come and sit round it on the logs, facing the flames, before they turn in for the night. The NEXT line suggests it once you have the cellar, a hand hired and $40.
- **The church, once mended,** has a use. At dawn its bell tolls three slow times, heard across the county and louder as you near town. Once a day, at its door, E has a word with the parson ("Go gently today, friend. Luck favours the patient pan."). ## The screen

On the crafting card and the build shelf, anything you're short of is inked in red, with how many you have, e.g. "4 stone (1)".

Everything on screen has its own place, and things queue rather than pile up:
- **Top-left:** the title, the clock (with the land you're in) and, out in the field, the way home. Under them sit the journal and the word round the county.
- **Top-centre:** one status line. "Paused" takes it over from the build hints.
- **Centre:** one panel at a time: crafting, the county map, your pack, the settlers card, the almanac, the calendar, letters and tales. Open another and the first waits, hidden, until the new one is put away, then comes back as you left it.
- **Top-right:** the ledger, a small paper tally of the major stuff you carry: gold, wood and stone always, and rope, clay, bricks, fibre, ore, iron, charcoal, steel and silver once you have some. Each count bumps when it changes. (The full pack is still on I.) It hides while a panel is open.
- **Bottom-centre, stacked upward so nothing overlaps:** the notes, then what E and the mouse do here, then the build shelf (in build mode), then the health, stamina and fullness bars, then the toolbar.

**Notes** (land names, lore, finds, warnings) sit in that bottom stack, so you can keep playing while you read. Two show at a time, each up long enough to read (longer for longer notes). The rest wait in a queue and take their turn as the earlier ones fold away. A short "+2 wood" never waits behind a story: it replaces the last short note, or jumps the queue. While a panel is open only the newest note shows.

## Days and nights

A day is 12 minutes. Deep night now runs three times as fast, so dusk to dawn takes about 2 minutes instead of 6. Dusk, dawn and the golden hours keep their own pace (and sleeping in a bed still skips the night).

## Hunger

Food matters now. A third bar under health and stamina shows how full you are. It empties over about a day and a half (twice as fast while you sprint), and a night's sleep leaves you hungry (-15).

- **Fed (30 and up):** wounds mend on their own, as before.
- **Peckish (under 30):** the bar turns rust and your stomach rumbles; wounds stop mending until you eat.
- **Starving (under 10):** the bar blinks red and your stamina is capped at half, so no long sprints.
- **Empty:** you slowly weaken, though never below 20 health; the farm is kind.

Eating (F) fills you by about four-fifths of what the dish heals, so hearty cooking (pie, honey cake, apple pie, johnnycakes) keeps you going far longer than a handful of berries. Fullness is saved with the game.

## Tool wear

Tools dull with use. Every blow with the axe, pickaxe or spear takes a little off its edge; better metal holds it longer (the old hatchet goes dull in about 80 blows, stone tools 100, iron about 165, steel nearly 300). A thin strip under each tool on the tool bar shows how sharp it is. A **dull** tool doesn't break, but it bites at half strength, and its slot says so. Sharpen everything at once on the grindstone at a **workbench or forge**: press E there (1 stone). Wear is saved with the game.

## Cold nights

After dark, out in the open and away from a fire, you're **chilled**: the clock says "chilly" (there's no screen effect). Chilled, wounds don't mend, you get your breath back at half speed, and you go hungry half as fast again. A **sheepskin coat** keeps the chill off on a clear night, though not on a snowy one. Stay out more than about 45 seconds on a snowy or wet night and it turns **bitter cold**: you slowly lose health (never below 20). A fire, a roof over your head, the porch rocker or your bed puts it right.

## Settlers and works

Folk you meet in the county can join the farm and run a work for you. What they make trickles into your storehouse a little at a time: enough to keep you going, never so much that you stop gathering yourself. You can watch it come in. Each settler fills a slatted crate on their back as they work: logs for wood, lumps of whatever else they make. With a crateful of three, or at dusk with whatever they have, they walk it over to the storehouse and tip it in, then go back to work. So a work built far from the storehouse brings in less. If the storehouse is full, they wait beside it with the crate. Anything in a crate is saved with the game. The settlers card (J) shows how full each crate is, and who's on their way with a load.

- **Works unlock as you gather.** The first time you gather a resource yourself, the work that makes it unlocks on the build menu's new **Works** shelf (B, then 5). For now that's the woodcutter's lot (wood), the stone quarry (stone), the fish weir (fish: a V of woven stakes funnelling into a wicker trap, a basket of fish on the bank) the clay pit (clay: a dug hollow of red clay, a heap with a spade stood in it, a stack of drying bricks) and the ore diggings (ore, once you've mined some at a guarded vein: a timbered adit into a heap of rock, a windlass, an ore cart of rust-red ore on a short run of track; 10 wood, 6 stone, 1 rope) and the bee yard (honey, once you've taken some from a hive: three straw skeps on a plank bench, sunflowers and a smoker; 8 wood, 8 fibre, 1 honey) and the grist mill (flour, the first of the river tier: it unlocks once you've gathered wheat, and must stand beside a creek or pond. A plank mill house with a shake roof, a flume and a big spoked water wheel; 14 wood, 8 stone) and the brick kiln (bricks: it unlocks with clay. A beehive dome of brick on a footing with an arched firebox glowing with embers, a squat chimney and a stack of fresh bricks; it glows warm after dark; 10 stone, 8 clay, 4 wood) and the sugar shack (syrup: it unlocks once you've tapped a maple for sap. A board-and-batten shed with a long vented cupola on the ridge, steam pouring out in paper puffs, stove wood by the door and sap buckets on a sled; 14 wood, 6 stone, 1 iron) and the tannery (leather: it unlocks once you've had a pelt. A hide laced taut on a pole frame, two tan vats of brown oak-bark liquor and a heap of bark; 12 wood, 4 stone, 2 rope) and the charcoal pit (charcoal: it unlocks once you've smelted iron. A low dome of turf smouldering over stacked wood, wisps of smoke from its vents, a rick of billets and a long rake; 16 wood, 4 stone, 4 clay). Each is a paper model: the lot has a chopping block with the axe bitten in, a sawbuck and a rick of split logs; the quarry has a cut rock face, squared blocks stacked to haul and a pick.
- **The storehouse** (Works shelf: 12 wood, 4 stone): a plank shed with a shake roof, a crate and a barrel by the door. It holds up to 30 of each thing your settlers make. Press E by it to take everything into your pack.
- **Settlers.** Once there's a storehouse, folk come looking for work and make camp, by a little fire, at the edge of the farm:
  - **Silas Crane**, a lanky woodcutter down on his luck, joins for 4 apples and 6 berries and runs a woodcutter's lot: about 1 wood every 45 seconds by day.
  - **Hattie Boone**, a broad-shouldered quarrywoman, joins for 8 wood and 6 berries and runs a stone quarry: about 1 stone a minute by day.
  
  - **Ada Hale**, a widow whose lean-to blew in with the last storm, joins if you **help** her: bring 10 wood and a coil of rope to mend it. She runs a fish weir: about 1 fish every 70 seconds by day.
  - **Big Jim Tuttle**, a mountain of a teamster, joins whoever can **beat him at arm-wrestling**. Press E to sit down across his crate, then press E as fast as you can (about five a second) to push his arm down before he pins yours; a bar in the hint shows who's winning. Lose and he's flexing his arm a while before you can try again. He runs a clay pit: about 1 clay a minute by day.

  - **Old Cyrus Pike**, a grizzled prospector with a white beard and a battered stovepipe, joins whoever can **out-tell him** at his fire after dark: you need three tall tales of your own (hear them by your campfire first). By day he just says tales are for the evening; with too few, he yawns and sends you off. He runs the ore diggings: about 1 ore every two minutes by day, a slow trickle, so the guarded veins are still where the real iron comes from.

Hire them before you build their work and they wait at camp. When someone joins, a note a moment later says plainly where they'll go: off to your work now, at first light if it's dark, or, if their work isn't built yet, that they're waiting by the camp fire until it stands. A note also reminds you if there's no storehouse to bring things to.
- **Feeding your settlers.** The storehouse has a larder. Press **R** by the storehouse to put plain food by as rations: fish, corn, apples, eggs and bread one to a ration, berries two (up to 60 rations). The settlers card shows the larder and how many days it will last.
- **The settlers card (J)** shows, at the top, what's in the storehouse and the larder, then who's camped, who's working where and at what rate, and who's hungry. Settlers, the storehouse's contents and what you've unlocked are saved with the game.

## The wild lands

**Every new game is a new world.** Each new game rolls its own seed, saved with the game, so the lakes, hills, wild lands, landmarks and treasure sites are different every time. In a new game the wild lands start close by the homestead: from about 60 m out, fully wild by about 115 m. A save from before this keeps its original world (seed 20261120) exactly as it was, with the wild lands starting around 130 m.

Out past the home county the world turns strange. Beyond the home county, broad wild lands begin, carved out of the endless map by slow noise fields. Walk far enough in any direction and you cross into one of them. Each has its own lie of the land, its own dye on the paper, its own trees, a mysterious treasure, and a creature from the old American tall tales that keeps it. There are no crop fields out there, and no warning signs.

- **The Silver Birch Hollows:** pale birch woods thick with toadstools. The treasure is **wild ginseng** ("sang"): bright, leafy plants with a glossy knot of red berries, a little bigger than the toadstools around them, picked with E. The keeper is the **Hidebehind**: you never see it, because it's always behind you, until it rears up tall and grey with lamp eyes.
- **The Redrock Breaks:** rust-dyed badlands of red boulders, dead snags and terraced rock. The treasure is **silver veins**: red rock split by broad bright seams, studded with nuggets and four-point glints. They need an iron pick or better. Two **sidehill gougers** keep each vein: shaggy rust-red beasts with short uphill legs and long downhill ones.
- **The Misty Fen:** a low wet flat of pools, hummocks, snags and cattails, with water dark as tea. True to its name, the fen is misty all day and night: the same torn tissue-paper sheets as the morning mist lie low over the pools there, tinted a cold grey-green. They never lift with the sun, thicken as you walk deeper in and thin out at its borders. The treasure is **mussel beds** at the water's edge, each holding a pearl (E). The keeper is the **Hodag**: horns, a row of spikes down its back, and a grin full of white teeth.
- **The Golden Prairie:** long dry swells of belt-high grass and wildflowers on gold ground. The treasure is **thunder eggs**, knobbly stones split open on violet crystal (pickaxe). A pack of three coyotes keeps each one.
- **The Bluepine Highlands:** lifted high and dry, dense blue-tinted timber. The treasure is **old pine stumps weeping amber** (axe). The keeper is the **wampus cat**, a long smoke-black panther with tawny bars and lamp-yellow eyes.

**Ambushes:** the Hodag and the wampus cat lie folded flat in the grass, and the Hidebehind isn't there at all. Come within a few strides of their treasure and they spring up on their hinges with a roar, then give chase. The first time you meet each one, a line from the old tales tells you what it is. Guarded treasure turns up at about one site in five chunks of wild country.

**Finding your way:** crossing into a land puts its name on the clock (for example "Day 11 · 10:20 · Misty Fen"). The first time you enter a land you get a note on what the old folks say of it; the lands you've found are saved with the game. The county map (M, or Tab) washes each land in its own colour, letters its name across its country like an old surveyor's map, and marks the treasure sites you've seen with a question mark. Out in the field, once you're more than about 90 m from the farm, a small paper chip appears beside the clock: a red house, an arrow that turns with your view to point home, and the distance ("Home, 200 paces"). Home is always marked: on the sheet it's a big red house on a cream badge with the distance ("Home, 150 paces"), drawn over every other mark so it never hides. When the homestead is off the edge of the sheet, a red inked arrow on the margin points the way home with the distance ("Home, 310 paces"). The little "home place" inset only shows while home is on the sheet.

**Landmarks:** each world has exactly one of each landmark, tall enough to see over the trees from a long way off: one arch, one steamboat, one lookout, one schooner and one Grandmother Birch. The seed picks a starting distance (about 130–330 m) and a bearing for each. From there the world searches outward, ring by ring, for a spot deep in that landmark's own land, with dry ground (the steamboat wants the water's edge) and at least 150 m from the others. That makes each one a one-time encounter, in a different place in every world.

**The Hermit of the Hollows** is a one-of-a-kind person, placed once per world deep in the Silver Birch Hollows. Old Asa is a white-bearded paper figure in a long patched coat, leaning on his staff by his fire. Behind him is a lean-to: a mossy bark roof sloping from a back wall of split boards up to two front poles, with a stump to sit on. He looks round at you as you come near. Reach him and he talks for an hour, inking every one-of-a-kind place in the world onto your county map (the arch, the wreck, the lookout, the lost wagon, the old birch, and where Babe grazes), then sends you off with a comb of honey. The rumour note knows of him: "They say an old hermit lives deep in the Hollows, and knows every strange corner of the county".

**Babe the Blue Ox** is the first one-of-a-kind *living* encounter, placed the same way: once per world, somewhere in the Bluepine Highlands. She's Paul Bunyan's own, a paper ox big as a barn and blue as a winter sky, with cream horns and a brass bell, grazing alone and swishing her tail. Walk up empty-handed and she snuffles at your pack; the hint comes back once a minute while you're near. Bring 3 apples and she takes them and leans her great head against you. From then on Paul Bunyan's blessing is with you: every tree you fell gives 1 extra wood (saved with the game). The rumour note can point you to her too: "Lumberjacks swear Babe the Blue Ox still grazes up in the high pines, partial to apples". Walk up to one for the first time and you find something there. Found landmarks are saved, and the county map marks them with a gold compass star (named once you've been there, a "?" before).
- **The Redrock Arch** (the Breaks): a natural bridge of banded red rock, built as three layered flats. Wedged in a crack is a prospector's poke with 1 silver and 2 iron.
- **The Belle of the Fen** (the Fen): a sternwheeler sunk to her rails and listing, with arched cabin windows, a pilot house, two crowned stacks (one leaning) and a rotting paddle wheel. The purser's strongbox holds 1 pearl, 3 rope and 2 honey.
- **The fire lookout** (the Highlands): a braced timber tower with a ladder, a catwalk and a windowed cabin under a red roof, with a flag. **Climb it:** stand at the foot of the ladder and press E ("[E] Climb the fire lookout"). The farmer goes up hand over hand to the catwalk, 9 m up, where you can walk round the cabin (the rail keeps you on). E at the head of the ladder climbs down. From the top you can see for miles, so the county map fills in for 150 m all round, and someone left 1 amber on the sill. (The view is only had from the top now.)
- **The lost schooner** (the Prairie): a broken-down covered wagon with a patched, torn canvas hood. One wheel lies fallen in the grass, and an ox skull bleaches nearby. Under the seat: 1 thunder egg, 2 leather and 1 pelt.
- **The Grandmother Birch** (the Hollows): a birch three times any other, in a ring of toadstools. Resting in her shade fully mends you, and among her roots are 2 sang and 1 honey.

**Climbing:** landmarks you can get up on are climbable (src/climb.js). Each registers decks you can walk on (flat, or following a curve) and ladders or scrambles between the ground and a deck. E at the foot climbs; E at the head comes down. Up there the ground-level colliders don't reach you, a railed deck keeps you on it, and from an open one you can walk off and drop.
- **The fire lookout:** the ladder up to the catwalk round the cabin.
- **The stone arch:** scramble up its west foot, walk over the crest about 6 m up, and drop off either end.
- **The farm windmill:** step round the stock tank onto the ladder up its south face and climb 8 m to the railed plank platform under the head, with the fan clattering overhead. The first climb each day lays the whole home place out below you: the county map is inked in for 110 m all round the farm.
- **The wreck of the Belle:** a boarding ladder at her bow from the water up to her listing main deck (railed), and a second ladder from there up to the hurricane deck on the cabin roof.

**Word of the landmarks:** when no journal goal needs a guarded site, the torn rumour note under the journal turns to hearsay about the nearest landmark you haven't visited yet, within about 320 m. It says how many paces off it is, and an inked arrow points the way. For example: "They say there's a fire lookout in the high pines, with a view for miles. About 270 paces that way." Once you've been there, the talk moves on to the next one.

**What it's worth:** the first of each treasure you gather comes with a word about it. The tinker buys them all:
- 2 silver for 1 steel and 2 iron.
- 2 pearls for 2 honey, 3 rope and 1 iron.
- 3 sang for 2 iron and 1 honey.
- 3 amber for 2 leather and 2 rope.
- 1 thunder egg for 1 iron and 1 pelt.

The wild lands' keepers drop pelts like the home guardians.

## The land, guardians and the tech tree

- **The land**
  - Gentle hills drawn as one smooth sheet of handmade paper.
  - Every 0.6 m of height is printed as a torn paper contour, with a white fibrous lip and a soft shadow, like a layered paper map.
  - The farm sits on its own level stretch. You walk straight through grass, bushes, flowers, toadstools, cattails, fallen apples and crops (trees, rocks and buildings still stop you). You can wade out from the banks a good way: shin-deep at the edge, sinking to the waist further out, going a bit over half pace in the shallows and slower the deeper you get; only the deepest water turns you back and slosh at every step. Every step sends a cream paper ripple ring spreading out from your shins, with a few flicks of paper spray, and standing still in the water a slower ring spreads every second or so (src/wake.js). The deep blue water further out still turns you back at its edge.
  - Ponds and lakes have clumps of cattails at the water's edge and notched lily pads in the shallows, a few with a cream-and-pink water lily in bloom.
- **Gathering**
  - Wood, stone and berries.
  - Fibre from tall grass, clay from riverbanks, and toadstools in the woods.
  - Old apple orchards stand here and there in the meadows: loose rows of green-crowned apple trees hung with red apples (chop them for wood), with heaps of windfall apples in the grass to pick. Eat apples raw, or bake an apple crisp (3 apples + 1 honey) at a campfire.
  - Iron ore and wild honey only at guarded sites. **Iron veins** lie in any high country, in the home lands and the wild lands alike (mostly the Bluepine Highlands and the Redrock Breaks), about one high chunk in ten. Each has a ring of rust-streaked ore rocks, an IRON VEIN sign and a pack of three coyotes. (They used to come only in the home lands' rare highland, so there was next to no iron in the game.) The **iron ore** rocks are the iron ore rock from the reference art, chunky grey stone banded with rusty seams. The **silver outcrops** are the silver rock, dark stone with bright white veins. Both are a little bigger than the cards they replace, so a vein is easy to spot. **The county map shows the iron:** the assayer has inked the veins he's heard of, so iron veins show on the map with crossed picks even in country you haven't explored. - **Rain showers:** every few minutes clouds roll in and a soft rain of paper slivers falls for a minute or two. The light greys and softens, the rain hisses and the birds go quiet; while it rains, everything regrows twice as fast and fish bite sooner. When a daytime shower ends, a rainbow of six muted paper bands hinges up out of the far fields on the side away from the sun, like the arch of a pop-up book; look up to see it. In a real downpour the town empties: the shopkeepers go in and shut their doors and the families of Squatters' Row walk to their tent flaps and duck inside (as they do at bedtime, rather than vanishing where they stand), coming out again when it eases (only the night trades, the Golden Garter and the Grand Hotel's clerk, keep their doors). - **Summer thunderstorms:** about one rain shower in four rolls in as a thunderstorm. It lasts longer and the light goes dark as a stovepipe, and the barn rooster warns of one coming. While it pours, every 7–21 seconds a cut-paper lightning bolt (a jagged card brighter than paper) stabs down out of the clouds somewhere far off across the county, flickers twice and is gone. The page flashes pale for an instant, and the thunder rolls in a beat or three later: the farther off the strike, the longer the wait and the softer the rumble. Snug on the porch, it's a fine show.
- **Snow days:** about a third of showers come as snow. Fat paper flakes drift and tumble down, and over a minute the land, roofs and anything facing the sky turn white. While snow lies on the ground the farmer leaves a winding trail of lilac-shadowed boot prints, which the thaw wipes away. On a snow day you can also build a snowman (build menu: 2 stone for the coal, 1 wood for the twig arms): two printed die-cut cards slotted crosswise, with three snowballs, twig arms, a battered hat, coal eyes and buttons, a carrot nose and a striped red scarf. It slumps away when the snow melts. When the snow lies thick, the ponds freeze over within half a minute and a note tells you so. The water turns to a pale blue ice sheet, scored with white skate loops and a few cracks, with the lily pads frozen in. You can walk right out onto it and skate: push off briskly (up to speed in about a second), let go and glide on a long way, and turn in wide sweeping curves. Your blades score a pair of pale scratches into the ice behind you, so a few loops leave figure-eights on the pond; they're wiped when it thaws. There's no fishing through the ice. After the snow melts the ponds thaw slowly, but never while you're standing on them. After the snow stops it melts away over a few minutes. Snow doesn't speed up the crops or the fish the way rain does. Once the page is white over, you leave a trail of footprints in it: little blue-grey boot prints, left and right a stride apart (hoofprints two by two when riding), which slowly fill in with fresh snow over half a minute or so. Old Blue leaves his own trail of little round paw prints, two by two, wherever he trots. In town, once the snow lies thick, two paper snowmen (top hat, red scarf, twig arms) stand up from the page on their hinges, one at the head of Main Street and one by Squatters' Row, and fold back down flat as the snow goes. Snow settles on roofs, ledges, boardwalks and the tops of rocks, but never on folk or beasts (the model figures and animals keep their own colours), so people and animals stand out clearly against the snow. The town's dirt streets take only a thin, churned cover, so they show as slushy tan bands with their ruts between the white boardwalks, and the county's footpaths stay trodden through it, a churned tan of slush along each lane, so the ways across the land still read when all the fields are white. The trees take it too: as the snow comes, the top of every tree's crown (pines, maples, aspens, oaks, apples, sage trees and birches) goes white along a ragged line, so the woods look snowed on and not just the ground. Your footsteps change with it, from the soft paper scuff to a muffled packed crunch.

On a building three squares wide, roof the outer rows and the ridge peaks the middle one. Aimed at anything else, it won't place. It keeps the rain off like a roof. Aim a floor at the edge of an upper floor and it carries on out from it at the same level (a balcony or a bigger upper storey); floors hung from one another this way have no legs either. A roof always sits on the tops of the walls round its cell, the highest storey you're aiming at.

## Life on the farm

- **The opening view:** the game starts close in over the farmer's shoulder (15 m out, about 26° down), from whichever side has the clearest line of sight. It weighs 24 directions round him, counting trees and buildings in the way heavily and low plants only near his feet, so it never opens on a trunk or a wildflower card filling the lens. Wheel out for the high diorama view.
- **Free camera:** mouse to look anywhere, from up at the sky to straight down; the wheel zooms from 2.5 to 42 (never far enough to see the world's edge). The view stays centred on the farmer, with no look-ahead, so it doesn't scoot sideways near trees, rocks or buildings. It eases up over hills instead of snapping. Trees and rocks standing between the camera and the farmer fade to a faint paper stipple so you can see him, and fill back in once you have passed.
- **Animals:** paper rabbits hop and deer graze, and both bolt when you get close. Hens potter around the farmyard, and in the open pasture south of the yard two honey-brown Guernsey cows (cream patches, a brass bell) and four black-faced sheep graze and amble about. On fine days, paper butterflies (cabbage whites, sulphurs, pale blues and the odd monarch) loop lazily over the grass around you, wings beating on their creases. They keep out of the rain and roost at dusk. Old Blue, the farm hound (a tan paper puppet with a dark saddle and floppy ears), is his own dog: rather than following your every step, he potters about within a few paces of you, ambling off to sniff a spot, sitting down on his haunches a while, then off to another every 6–16 seconds, and only lopes after you once you're about 14 m off, wags when you're close (press E by him for a scratch behind the ears: he wriggles, thumps his tail and woofs, and half the time drops a slobbery stick at your feet. Press E by the stick to throw it: it sails off end over end, he's after it like a shot, and he gallops back with it crosswise in his mouth to drop it at your feet again. Leave it lying a while and he takes it off to chew. Once a day he wanders a few steps off, digs with his nose down and dirt flying, and trots back to drop a find at your feet: an old horseshoe (iron), lost rope, a jar of honey, toadstools, an old fur cap (a pelt), or just a very fine stick he's extremely proud of. He never fights, and if he's left far behind he lopes back to catch up. He gives a gruff double woof when a guardian or the crows are about. Marmalade, the barn cat (a ginger-tabby paper puppet with a cream bib and a question-mark tail), keeps her own hours. Press E by her for a scratch under the chin and she purrs. By day she naps in a loaf, paws tucked under and ears flicking, on her favourite spots: the top of the hay bales (she hops up and down with a springy bounce), the porch by the rocker, a warm patch of yard by the garden, and under the washing line. Every minute or so she strolls off to another, slipping round the farmhouse, barn, bales and fences rather than through them. After dark she goes mousing along the garden rows, low and slow. She keeps clear of Old Blue. After dark a paper owl with ear tufts and amber eyes glides in to the corner post of the pen. It turns its head after you, tilts it slowly, hoots softly now and then, and flies off at dawn. Walk up and press E to milk a cow (once a day each) or shear a sheep (2–3 wool; she looks slimmer until her fleece grows back two days later). Milk and 2 corn make buttermilk biscuits at a campfire; the tinker takes 3 wool for a jar of honey and a coil of rope. A flock of three or four wild turkeys pecks through the woods too (bronze, barred with buff, a bare red head and the tail fanned up behind), off at a run if you come close; a bow, rifle or revolver can take one for two cuts of turkey (kept with your venison). Within earshot of the flock you hear a bubbling gobble now and then from the woods.
- **Washing day:** a clothesline strung between two T-posts east of the yard holds a buffalo-plaid flannel shirt, a pair of denim overalls with yellow stitching, a patchwork quilt with a barn star, two red-toed wool socks and a striped tea towel. Each is a printed die-cut card with its clothespins cut into the top edge, and each swings on the twine as on a crease: lazily in the breeze and further in a gust. In the rain the washing soaks through and hangs heavy and nearly still, then dries out slowly afterwards.
- **Pond frogs:** little die-cut paper frogs (moss green with dark spots, a cream chin and gold eyes) squat on every other lily pad near you. Walk within a few steps and a frog leaps off in a high arc and plops into the water, sending out a pair of spreading rings. About 20 seconds later it hinges back up onto its pad, once you've moved off. From dusk and through the night they croak now and then (quieter the further off they are), and they stay out of sight while snow lies on the ground. All the frogs are one instanced mesh and the ripples another.
- **A duck and her ducklings:** by day, on a pond near you, a brown paper mallard paddles slow loops with three or four downy yellow ducklings strung out behind her in a line, each following the one in front and bobbing on the ripples, with little rings of wake spreading behind her and a nasal quack now and then. Come close and she hurries them off round the pond; they're off the water at night and in a downpour. Old Blue can't leave them be: now and then he lopes down to the bank barking, and she hurries her brood off round the pond until he gives it up. Stand on the bank with a cob of corn and press E to toss it on the water: she quacks to her brood and they come paddling over in a line to dabble at it a while, close by you.
- **Paper kite** (workbench: 4 fibre, 1 wood, 1 rope): a patchwork diamond of barn red, cream and blue on two cross-sticks, with a cream star sewn in the middle and a tail of little paper bows. Press G to let it up: it climbs with a springy rise and rides high on the wind, bobbing and rocking on the gusts and snaking its tail, on a sagging twine string from the farmer's hand. It follows as you walk, and always flies out to the side where the camera can see it. G reels it in, and a shower brings it fluttering down. While it's up within about 30 m of the kitchen garden, crows won't come, and any already pecking scatter, so it makes a portable scarecrow.
- **Lightning-bug jar** (workbench: 1 honey, 1 rope; you eat the honey, scrape the jar and punch the lid): the farmer carries it in his left hand. After dark, stand by a firefly and press E to catch it. Each one makes the jar glow warmer and throw more soft, flickering light around you, up to six, so it doubles as a lantern for night walks. At first light he unscrews the lid and lets them all go home.
- **Geese going over:** every few minutes by day a skein of seven Canada geese crosses the sky in a V, some 16 m up and passing near overhead, headed more or less south. Each is built to read from above: a grey-brown back with a pale breast, a black neck and head with the white chinstrap, and two broad wings beating slowly, each bird a beat out of step. They honk softly now and then, their shadows slide over the land, and the first flight brings a note (an early flight means an early frost).
- **Bison on the high prairie:** out on the open highland a herd of four or five bison grazes around one spot, a paper puppet each: a towering shoulder hump in a shaggy dark-brown cape printed with curls of hair, a lighter tan rump, a low heavy head with a beard and short cream horns, short stout legs and a tufted tail. They amble and graze; crowd them (closer than about 6 m) and they lumber off. The first time you come on the herd, a note tells of the days they ran so thick the ground shook. There is one herd at a time, and it is left behind when you go far off.
- **Fallen logs:** the woods floor is wilder now. Here and there in the forest (never on the lanes) lies a fallen log: a rolled bark tube with long furrows and lichen flecks, pale end-grain rings with a rot-dark heart at each cut end, a torn sheet of moss draped over its back, tiers of tan shelf fungus on one flank and a fern at its foot. You can't walk through them. They are one instanced mesh with a single painted texture, so they cost one draw call.
- **Snags and a bald eagle:** dead snags stand about the highland and now and then in the woods: weathered silver trunks with a jagged broken top, stubby limbs reaching up, grain cracks, lichen and a woodpecker hole or two (chop one for 2-3 wood). Over the high country, by day, a bald eagle soars in wide slow circles some 26 m up, banked into the turn, with dark-brown fingered wings, a white head and tail and a yellow beak; now and then it gives three lazy wingbeats, and its shadow sweeps the hillside.
- **A coyote on the rise:** now and then on a dark, dry night out in the open country (well away from the farm and the town), a coyote trots up onto the highest ground nearby, sits on its haunches side-on against the sky, tips its nose up and howls, wavering and falling away into a couple of yips. Far off another answers, then it gets up and lopes away over the rise. It is only scenery and never comes for you.
- **Beavers:** out in the shallows of the bigger ponds (open water all round, the bank a few strides off) stands a beaver lodge: a lumpy dome of gnawed sticks and mud daubs, pale where the bark is chewed off. Near the farmer, a paper beaver swims slow loops round its lodge, broad brown back and head just showing, a dark paddle of a tail trailing and a pale V of wake spreading behind. Every half minute or so it slaps its tail with a double plop and dives, and comes up again further round. One beaver at a time, at the lodge nearest you.
- **White birches:** stands of white birch grow among the pines and maples (and here and there in the meadow): a slim chalk-white trunk printed with black bark dashes and dark knots, a few thin dark branches, and a narrow oval crown of small butter-yellow and spring-green leaves, standing a little taller than the round trees. Chop one for 2-3 wood; it grows back like the rest.
- **Wildflowers:** the flower clumps in the meadows and woods are a loose handful of prairie wildflowers, each on its own stem out of a low leafy tuft, with sky between them: purple coneflowers (pink petals hanging from a copper cone), black-eyed Susans (gold round a dark heart) and little blue asters. The stems and round heads are the cut outline.
- **Dandelion clocks:** little clumps of seeded dandelions stand about the meadows (never in the lanes): crossed die-cut cards with a sage stem and broad toothed leaves under a cream puff ball printed with a ring of seeds. Walk through a clump, or let Old Blue barrel through it, and the clocks you brushed puff away: a dozen tiny paper parachutes each rise and drift off east on the west wind, turning slowly, and the clock is left a bald green nub. Folks say count the puffs to tell the hour; a note gives the dandelion's guess (once in a while it's right on the dot: make a wish). They seed again by the next morning.
- **Leaf piles:** four raked heaps of die-cut maple leaves (orange, barn red, mustard and rust) sit round the yard. Run through one, or let Old Blue barrel into it, and with a dry rustle the whole heap bursts up and away from you. The leaves spin, flutter down and lie flat and scattered where they land. About half a minute later the breeze sweeps them back over the grass, one hop at a time, into a heap again. It's one instanced mesh that only updates while leaves are moving.
- **Ambient:** printed leaves flutter down by day and fireflies come out at night. Trees, bushes and grass sway in the wind.
- **No chores:** there's no task list. The old journal card of goals (milk the cow, fell five trees and so on) is retired, and nothing pays out for doing things on order. Everything is simply there to do when you feel like it: milk Daisy, fish, build, wander off to the high prairie. What you get up to is still tallied, and the almanac reads your day back when you sleep.

## Crafting & building

- **Tool bar:** the bottom of the screen holds your tools, not your materials: 1 hands, 2 axe (the old hatchet you start with, then the stone and iron axe in the same slot), 3 pickaxe, 4 ash spear, 5 fishing pole. Tools you haven't made yet show greyed with a "?". The one in hand is raised on a mustard card and shows in the farmer's hand. The right tool matters: trees want the axe, iron veins the pickaxe (rock gives a few chips to anything, but splits fast under a pick), and the spear hits guardians hardest and reaches farther. Berries, grass, toadstools, corn, pumpkins and wheat come away with anything, bare hands included. Or just press E at one: E picks the whole thing at once (berries, toadstools, an armful of grass, windfall apples, wheat sheaves, sweet corn, a pumpkin, a wild hive's honey, a lost satchel), whatever's in your hand. E only gathers when nothing else nearby wants it (the dog, a door, a woodpile and so on come first). Swing the wrong tool and the hint says which number to press. The fishing pole has to be in hand to cast. Wood, stone, food and the rest live in your pack (I), and every gather still pops up a "+3 wood" note.
- **Crafting** (C): hammer (unlocks building), stone axe (2× chop), pickaxe (3× mining), berry basket (+1 berry per bush).
- **Building** (B, needs the hammer):
  - Pieces sit on four shelves: Workshop (workbench, campfire, forge, cider press), Cabin (floor, wall, doorway, stone wall, roof, stairs, bed), Yard (fence, lantern, scarecrow, dock, skep, sapling, bird feeder, woodpile, giant-pumpkin patch) and Odds & ends (jack-o'-lantern, snowman, trophy). Number keys 1–4 open a shelf; Q/E step through every piece.
  - Snapping: floors and roofs snap to a 2 m grid, and walls snap to grid edges.
  - Structural pieces need a workbench within 20 m.
  - Floors and stairs are walkable, and walls and fences block movement.
  - Removing a piece refunds it in full.
- **Breaking builds**: swing at a built piece to break it and get its materials back. Stone pieces break faster with the pickaxe.
- **Campfires** double stamina regen nearby. Soft cream paper smoke puffs (five round lobes, cut from one sheet) curl up from each campfire and from the forge's chimney, leaning with the west wind and opening out as they climb. They're thinner in the rain. After dusk, little orange flecks of cut paper twist up out of the coals and wink out. Only the three fires nearest you get them; it's two instanced meshes in all. Lanterns glow after dusk.

## Sound

Everything is synthesized with WebAudio in `src/audio.js`; there are no sample files.
- **Paper foley**: footsteps (paper on grass, a hollow knock on boards), chopping, mining, picking, tree falls (a quickening crackle of splitting fibres, a whoosh of leaves and a soft toneless thud; the old low, buzzy creak sounded rude), building (a fold "fwip"), tears, crafting chimes.
- **Ambience**: wind, shoreline water, birds by day, a far-off cricket trilling softly now and then at night (it used to buzz every second), campfire crackle.
- **Each wild land sounds like itself.** The wind changes: a dry, high hiss in the Redrock Breaks, a long roll across the prairie, a low soughing in the Bluepine Highlands, hardly a breath in the Misty Fen. Songbirds thin out in the wilds. Every 5–14 seconds the land adds a voice of its own, panned and softened with distance:
  - The fen: green-frog "gunks" (bullfrogs by night) and drips into black water.
  - The Breaks: a red-tail hawk's falling scream, and grit trickling down a rock face.
  - The prairie: a meadowlark's tumbling song and grasshopper rasps by day, crickets by night.
  - The highlands: a woodpecker's far drumroll, ravens, and owls after dark.
  - The Hollows: a wood thrush's "ee-o-lay" by day, and now and then a twig snapping somewhere close behind you, then nothing.
- **Music**: a lone Appalachian clawhammer banjo, out in front, in the old mountain-minor and modal keys, with a loping western gait: four short original tunes chosen by the time of day: "Hollow Morning" in G minor, "Long Field" (a dorian waltz in D) through the afternoon, "Crow's Lament" in E phrygian at dusk and "Hush, the Lantern's Low", a graveside lullaby in A minor, after dark. Every tune is frailed bum-ba-ditty: the melody note, a dry brush of the back of the nail across the strings, then the thumb on the high fifth-string drone, the ditty coming round again on long notes. A bright, nasal plunk over a low bass and a dry strum; a fiddle drones quietly underneath and sighs along on the longest notes. Long quiet spells between tunes. All synthesized.
- **Mixing**: a soft limiter instead of a compressor, kept quiet and sparse following the papercraft skill's sound guidance. M toggles sound.
- **Postcards (K):** snaps the view as it is, pasted onto a cream card with a deckled edge, the caption "Greetings from Paperfjord" with the day and time, and a red 2¢ stamp. It's saved as `paperfjord-dayN.png` in your downloads.
- **A quiet HUD:** the health, stamina and fullness bars fade away after a few seconds of all being well (fit, rested, fed and warm), and are back the instant one of them matters. The renderer and frame-rate badge and the time-of-day buttons are developer tools now, hidden unless you press **F3** (it remembers). When a new storehouse brings several folk at once, you get one note saying so instead of a note per settler.
- **Notes** (bottom right): little paper notes pinned up by hand, each a touch crooked and wrapped to a neat column so they never sprawl across the screen. Long ones stay up long enough to read (about two seconds plus a beat per word), the same note twice just stays up longer instead of stacking, and at most three are pinned at once: a fourth nudges the oldest away early.
- **Hints** (above the pack bar): two lines when both apply. On top is what E does here (sleep, tell a tale, milk, shear, take honey, gather eggs, trade); below is what the mouse does to what you're facing (chop, pick, mine, fight, break).
- **Pack bar** (bottom): one paper tag per material you carry. All cooked dishes share a single pantry basket, which shows how many dishes you have and the one F will eat next. Tags shrink when the bar gets crowded.
- **Saving**: builds, inventory, tools, beaten guardians, the calendar (day and time), the map you've explored, the mail, the fair, the tales you've heard, your giant pumpkins and the rest are saved in `localStorage` (`paperfjord.builds.v1`). The game autosaves every 30 seconds and when you leave the page, so a reload picks up on the same day, hour and tinker schedule.

## Look

- **A strong, directional sun:** by day the sun is the main light (up about a quarter), and the sky ambient and the frontal fill are turned down (about 27 % and 30 %). Every tree, fence and figure now throws a long, dark, readable shadow, and the shade under the woods reads deep. Nights keep their old moonlit fill.
- **Sunbeams through the trees** (src/shafts.js): soft shafts of light cut from tissue paper slant down toward the ground along the sun's direction, streaked with paler fibre threads and tinted with the sun's colour. A dozen drift around the farmer, mostly beside trees, each fading in and out on its own slow 7–16 second clock. They're strongest in the low gold of morning and evening, softer at noon, fainter in the open than in the woods, and gone at night and in the rain. A beam passing close to the lens fades out rather than washing the frame. It's all one instanced mesh, one draw call, and the frame time measured the same with or without them.
- **Patchwork fields:** the county's crop fields are a quilt of patches about 40 m across, each with its rows running its own way and drawn slightly wavy, by hand. Some are golden wheat (darker drill rows, pale glints of the heads), some fresh-ploughed earth (brown furrows, each with a lit ridge and a shadowed trough) and some mown hay (broad sage swathes, light and dark where the mower turned). Every patch has a torn cream paper border where it meets the next. The patches themselves are painted in the ground shader. They're dressed to match: round hay bales (a rolled straw spiral with a red twine band; you can't walk through them) lie about the mown hay, and wheat stooks (sheaves stood upright, tied at the waist, heads fanned on top) stand in short rows of two or three on the wheat. Click a stook to gather 2–3 wheat; it stands back up after about four minutes. At a campfire, 3 wheat and an egg bake a farmhouse loaf (heals 35). On his visiting days, the tinker takes 6 wheat ("flour for the mill") for a bar of iron and a coil of rope. The world reads the same noise texels as the shader, so the dressing always lands on the right kind of patch.
- **Ponds and lakes:** water is a teal-blue sheet, deeper in the middle, with short pale painted ripple strokes drifting slowly across it. The bank is a torn sheet lying on the water, with a ragged cream paper edge and a soft shadow just off it. Forest, field and highland sheets are printed on land only, never across the water. (They used to cover many ponds, and the warm grade had turned the old pale teal khaki, so ponds read as bare ground.)
- **The night sky:** after dark, seventy die-cut paper stars (cream and old gold, each with printed rays and a cut edge) turn in on their creases one by one, from edge-on to facing you, with a little quiver at the end. Slowly rocking, they hang far out on the sky. A paper crescent moon with a dozing man-in-the-moon face rides opposite the sun, with a soft glow on the sky around it. At dawn they all turn away again. Look up with the free camera to see them. They're one instanced mesh plus one card, and they only exist at night.
- **The harvest moon:** on the night before each County Fair (Day 6, 13, 20…), the usual paper crescent gives way to a big full harvest moon. It's a round card tinted pumpkin-gold, with craters and the man in the moon dozing with a broad smile, hanging lower over the fields. A note says it's rising, and the almanac warns you the day before. Folks say whatever you gather under it comes in double-handed, and so it does: every bush, crop, tree or rock you gather after dark that night yields one extra.
- **Falling stars:** every few minutes on a clear night, a paper star breaks loose, rocks its way down the sky trailing a gold paper ribbon, and lands 20–40 m from you. There it hinges up out of the grass, turning slowly and throwing a warm glow on the ground. A note says which way it fell ("off to the north-west, not far"). Walk out (the lightning-bug jar helps) and press E by it to take it home: it leaves a little luck behind, such as honey, star-iron, wool and rope, a breakfast of trout and eggs, apples and berries, or a honey cake. Unfound stars fade at first light.
- **Morning mist:** most mornings, and every morning after a night of rain, long torn sheets of cream tissue paper lie a metre or two above the fields at first light. Their edges are soft, and you can see the land, the fence tops and the bushes through them. They drift slowly with the breeze and, from about 07:00, lift away and thin out until they're gone by half past nine. That's 14 sheets around the farmer, in one instanced mesh.
- **The title card:** the pause card opens on a little torn-paper vignette (an inline SVG with a slight torn-edge wobble). It shows layered hills in sage, gold and brown, the red barn, pines and an orange tree, the windmill, a cream fence, and a gold sun, all with a brown cut edge. Under it sit the name and a new subtitle ("a folk-tale farm, cut from paper and stood up on its hinges"), with the keys set in two tidy columns (one on a narrow screen).
- **Cloud shadows:** on fair days, big soft blots of shade drift slowly across the fields with the wind. They fade under rain clouds and at night.
- **Everything is paper:** built pieces follow the same rule as the props. The campfire is a ring of die-cut stone cards around printed log cards and a paper flame; the bee skep and the snowman are printed cards slotted crosswise.

- **Paper & watercolour**: all textures are painted procedurally on canvases at load (`textures.js`). They include tiling paper fibre, watercolour bloom washes, and illustrated card art for trees and bushes, buildings and clouds. Materials (`materials.js`) add fibre bump, world-space washes, torn-paper paths and shoreline foam.
- **Post** (`post.js`, a TSL `RenderPipeline`):
  - effects: GTAO, a hand-painted UV wobble, watercolour edge pooling, god rays, tilt-shift/depth of field, bloom
  - a vintage grade, screen-locked paper grain, vignette and film grain
- **Lighting** (`sky.js`): sun/moon key light, sky ambient and a frontal fill whose colour follows the mood (warm day, cool golden, violet dusk, blue night). Ambient rises after dark, so night stays readable. Exposure lifts gently at night (`main.js`).
- **Atmosphere** (`sky.js`): a watercolour sky dome, paper-card clouds, and a fog node with valley mist that glows toward the sun. The day/night cycle also drives the lanterns and window glow.
- **Grade tuning**: the grade numbers (exposure, contrast, saturation, shadow warmth) were fitted by measuring Lab lightness percentiles, chroma and shadow and midtone hue against a reference frame, not by eye.

## Performance

- **Town folk sleep when you're away:** a skinned figure's bounds don't follow its pose, so each model figure is given its bind-pose bounding sphere made half again as big (room for a swung arm or a kick) and is view-culled with that: folk behind the camera or off the page aren't drawn in the main pass (in a typical view down the street about four in five are skipped). When the camera is more than 130 m from Paydirt Hollow, its 30-odd townsfolk (keepers, the Row, the dancers, the opera queue, the revue) are hidden and their animation skipped; they're back as you ride in. The native camps already do the same beyond 140 m. So do your settlers: one more than 130 m from the camera is hidden and not animated, though they carry on at their work (and haul their crates) all the same. The flower barrels by the keepers' doors share their paper and are baked down by colour, five meshes a barrel instead of nine.

- **Graphics quality (O):** auto, high, medium or low. Auto steps down if the frame rate stays under 50. On medium the sun's shadow map is redrawn every second frame, and on low every third, but only while the view is still (while the camera moves, shadows redraw every frame so they never hop). It saves a whole render pass. Trees that fade out of the camera's way are drawn by a small separate mesh, so the thousands of ordinary trees carry no per-pixel test. Paper animals are hidden past 55 m and drop their shadows past 22 m, and six rabbits roam at a time.
- **Frame cost, re-measured** (with everything up to the bird feeder, Chromium, 1200×800, farm in view): about 0.8 ms of CPU by day and 1.0 ms by night per frame, including submitting the render, and about 4–5 ms per frame in total once the GPU work is done; roughly 180 draw calls.
- **Lamps by night only:** each of the 14 point lights (the porch, four lanterns, six build-light slots, the lightning-bug jar, a fallen star and the barn dance's lantern string) costs a loop in every lit pixel's shader, even at zero brightness. By day they're all hidden, and they switch back on at dusk (with a little hysteresis). So there are exactly two lighting set-ups, day and night, and both are compiled at load, so dusk never hitches. In Chromium at 1200×800 this saves about 0.3 ms of GPU per daytime frame (≈8 %). A campfire no longer throws its own light by day; it was lost in the sunlight anyway. CPU cost per frame, measured with every system's update timed, is about 0.2 ms; the critters and ambient life cost the most, at about 0.02 ms each.
- **Fewer draw calls:** props are instanced. The homestead, nesting box and tinker's wagon are each merged into one mesh per material.
- **Distance detail for animals:**
  - Paper animals more than 30 m from the camera drop their shadows.
  - Animals beyond 70 m are hidden.

- **Where the time goes** (measured in Chromium at 1200×800, raining, farm in view):
  - About 0.6 ms of CPU per frame across every system. Particles, critters and rendering submit lead, and nothing is over 0.1 ms.
  - About 3 ms including the GPU.
  - On a slow machine the GPU is the place to save: press O for medium or low quality.
- **A stable world:** each map chunk's layout (props, guarded sites, orchards, satchels) is drawn from a fixed random stream, so it comes out the same whether it's generated far away for the map, the rumours and the wisp, or up close when you walk in.
- **Walkers go round things:** Old Blue, the lost calf and Johnny Appleseed share a little steering helper (`steer.js`), and Marmalade has her own. It bends their heading to slip round the nearer side of any building, bale, wagon or fence post ahead, and nudges them back out if they ever end up inside one. Obstacles nearby are re-gathered only twice a second, so it costs next to nothing. In a test, Johnny's whole walk across the farm kept at least 0.6 m clear of everything, and Old Blue followed the farmer right round the barn without cutting through it.
- **Never freezes on a bug:** if something goes wrong in a frame, the game logs it to the console (a few times at most), shows one "little tear in the paper" toast and keeps running, rather than stopping dead.

## High country: valleys between the ranges

Gold country is high country. The whole county is a web of connected valleys between great mountain ridges (src/world.js, `rangeAt`). The ridges run in long broken lines with gaps between, so you can ride out in most directions and go round any mountain, through a gap or along a valley. The home valley lies clear for about 150-200 m round the farm and the town, and past the last valleys, about 950 m out, an outer wall closes the world (the ridges stand well apart, so the valleys are big). The mountains stand about 30 m above the valley floor with steep sides and rounded summits; you (and Biscuit) can walk up only the foot of a slope, never the mountainside.

- **Their look:** folded paper. Each mountain's height lives on a coarse 6 m lattice and runs flat between its nodes, so every slope is a big flat facet with a sharp crease, catching the light on its own (src/world.js `mtnLattice`). Handmade paper dyed cold blue-grey, deep at the foot and paling upward, then white snow above a ragged snow line; no lines or stripes. Far off they fade toward a cold pale blue.
- **Near and far alike:** one mesh of every range in the world (src/ranges.js), built once on the same lattice, is the mountain both near and far; the detailed ground under it follows the same facets, for walking on, so nothing pops, swaps or shows through.
- Guarded sites, landmarks, lost satchels and the town never land up in the mountains.

## Guns

- **The six-shooter** ($30 at the general store, with 12 cartridges) and **the buffalo rifle** ($55, with 6) go on the tool bar after the bow (keys 7 and 8). They work like the bow: hold the mouse button to raise the gun and pan the camera to put the reticle on your mark, then let go to fire. He carries a gun in his fist at his side, barrel ahead and down, and raises it level while aiming. The guns ride in his body's frame at the hand, so they always point where he faces. A shot is a lead slug with a pale streak behind it, so you can see it fly; it doesn't stick in anything. Each shot coughs out a puff of powder smoke: three lobed scraps of torn cream paper with a thin brown cut edge. They billow out ahead of the muzzle, slow, drift upward turning, and thin away in about a second (a bigger, longer cloud from the rifle). Eighteen scraps are cut once and reused, so a gunfight costs nothing extra.
- **The six-shooter** is up in a third of a second and good to about 30 m. Six in the cylinder; when it's empty it reloads from your cartridges in about 2.4 seconds (R reloads early). One shot does a little less than an arrow (two or so bring a warrior down).
- **The rifle** takes about a second to settle and reaches 75 m. One shot, then a 1.6-second reload. A hit drops a warrior or a buck outright.
- **Cartridges** come in boxes of 12 for $2 at the general store and show in the ledger. Out of them, the gun just clicks. Both guns work from the saddle.

## The people of the valleys

There are three camps of the valley people, each in an open valley of its own, at least 180 m from the next: one a good ride out (240-340 m from the farm), one further (400-560 m) and one far off (560-760 m). Each has its own people, horses and land, and keeps its own watch: trespass on one and only that camp turns out. Each is a camp of the valley people: lodges round a campfire, a horse picket with three horses tied along a rail, and their land marked out by a ring of painted poles (red and white bands, two feathers) about 46 m round it (src/natives.js). The county map shows each, a dashed red ring marked "Their land"; when none is on the sheet, a red arrow on the margin points to the nearest with its distance.

- **Who lives there** (from the reference art): three warriors, each armed as in the art, the warrior with a spear and a bow, the archer with his bow, the hunter with a stone-headed club; and the camp's folk, who slip back among the lodges when there's fighting: the woman, between the lodges and the fire; the grey-braided elder with her walking staff, who mostly sits by the fire; and the girl, who skips about the camp and is often off to see the horses at the picket. Two more folk live there now: a young woman in a pleated green dress with braids and fringed moccasins, who wanders between the lodges, and a grandfather in a fringed buckskin shirt and a green cape, who mostly sits by the fire. They never fight.
- **Trespass:** the prospector is the stranger here. Cross the poles and a warrior runs out, signals you to turn back and puts an arrow in the ground at your feet. Stay a few seconds, or push on toward the lodges, and it's a fight.
- **The fight:** the bowmen run to their horses, mount and ride at you, circling at bowshot and loosing from the saddle; the hunter closes in on foot with his club. Step back over the poles and they break off, ride back to the picket and dismount.
- **Fighting back:** your arrows strike a warrior they pass through (aim the bow's reticle at him and it turns gold), and your blows land on one in front of you. About two arrows bring a warrior down: he falls on the grass and lies there a while; his horse bolts, then wanders back to the picket. Striking one anywhere starts a fight that follows you a good way off their land. A fallen warrior is back with his people another day. One shot from the saddle drops to the grass, not where he sat.
- **Your own horse:** you can draw and shoot from Biscuit's saddle too.
- **The camp** is the reference-art models now: plain and painted tipis, a cookfire, drying rack, travois, drum and bedroll. Each camp stands in a clearing: no tree, bush or rock grows within about 34 m of its fire.
- **Gifts and peace:** at a camp's ring of painted poles, press E to leave a gift (two cuts of venison, a jar of honey or a good hide). The elder accepts it, and you are welcome on their land for three days: no warning, no arrows, walk right up to the fire. The girl runs out with a coil of braided rawhide (+2 rope). The E prompt at the poles shows how many days are left. Strike one of them and the peace is over at once. Peace is saved with the game. While you're at peace with the camp nearest town, no war party rides from it to raid Paydirt Hollow. The county map labels a friendly camp "Friends: welcome N days" instead of "Their land" (and its edge-of-sheet arrow says "Friends"). While you're welcome, press E by their fire to trade: beaded moccasins for 2 hides (as good as boots: sprinting tires you a third less), 3 coils of braided rawhide for a cut of venison, 2 tanned hides for a bar of iron, and a dried root of wild sang for a jar of honey.
- **Night in the camps:** after dark a fire burns low in every lodge, and the hide glows warm from within with its painted bands showing through and a faint flicker (one shared shader uniform, no extra lights). From late evening till first light the camp's folk are abed in the lodges; the warriors keep watch. Their cookfire sends up the same cream paper smoke puffs as your own campfires (with sparks after dusk), and so does the bunkhouse supper fire while it's lit; as before, only the three fires nearest you smoke.
- **In a fight, the camp's folk stay in sight.** The woman, the elder and the girl hurry (not flee) to stand beside a lodge on the far side from the trouble, out in the open, rather than vanishing inside.
- **Raids on the town:** every 3 to 6 days, by daylight and only while you're within about 220 m of town, a war party of three rides down out of the hills from the nearest camp, making for one of the mended buildings (never the church). A toast names the building. They circle it, whooping and shooting at you if you come within bowshot. Bring down two of the three (arrows, bullets or blows) and the last one wheels away; the town thanks you with $10. If they circle for about 22 seconds, they wreck the place: it stands run-down again, its keeper gone, until you mend it again at half the cost.
